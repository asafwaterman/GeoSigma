How the surveys measured resistivity

The reports on this map use four method families. Each summary below is taken from the cited literature and from the survey reports themselves; numbers in brackets point to the references. Conductivity σ is the inverse of resistivity ρ (σ = 1/ρ).

DC resistivity Magnetotellurics (MT) CSAMT TDEM References

DC resistivity (VES, profiling, dipole mapping)

Principle. A direct current is injected through one pair of electrodes and the potential difference is measured with a second pair. The surface potential depends on how resistivity is distributed below, so the measurements are converted to apparent resistivity (the resistivity of a uniform half-space that would give the same reading) and then inverted to true resistivity versus depth [1].

Measurement. Depth is gained by widening the electrode separation, which should be several times (at least about 3×) the target depth [1]. Wenner moves all four electrodes at a fixed equal spacing (best signal, slowest); Schlumberger moves only the current electrodes (more depth per cable length, faster); dipole-dipole needs the least cable but has the weakest signal [1]. Multi-electrode 2-D/3-D imaging resolves structures that 1-D soundings cannot [2].

Uses and limits. Simple, cheap and widely used for groundwater, environmental, engineering and mining work [1, 2]; in Israel mainly to map aquifers and saline water [1]. Grounding is hard in dry sand or hard rock, long arrays lower lateral resolution, and arrays that cross faults mix blocks and mislead 1-D interpretation [1].

On this map. VES: Schlumberger soundings (Paparo, Carmel) and modified-Schlumberger and monopole soundings, whose probing depth is taken as the distance OB [R1]. Profiling: fixed-spacing Wenner readings repeated over time (Geoelectric Atlas). Roving dipole (Yizreel-Harod): a fixed 2-3 km source dipole and L-shaped receiver dipoles moved around it give an apparent-resistivity map for fast reconnaissance of large conductive bodies [R1]; the array is easy to run but sensitive to small lateral inhomogeneities [3]. Dipole-dipole pseudo-sections plot separation, not depth [R1]. Neither gives an author model, so their readings are not shown.

Magnetotellurics (MT)

Principle. Natural electromagnetic variations, from the ionosphere and from lightning, induce currents in the Earth [6]; below about 1 Hz the source is mainly ionospheric and above it worldwide thunderstorms [5]. The ratio of orthogonal horizontal electric and magnetic fields, with their phase, is diagnostic of the true resistivities of the layers below [4]. Penetration grows with period and falls with conductivity, so long periods see deeper [4, 5].

Measurement. E is recorded with non-polarising electrodes and B with induction coils; the impedance tensor computed from them is shown as apparent resistivity and phase versus period [6]. Cagniard's form is ρa = 0.2·T·|E/H|² (T in s, E in mV/km, H in gamma = nT) [R2]. Klang's IPRG equipment covered 0.002-150 Hz [R2]; the 2006 Dead Sea transect 1 kHz-1 mHz [6]. Simple layered interpretation assumes plane waves and a horizontally layered earth [R2].

Uses and limits. Needs no current source or long cables and reaches great depth [4]; suited to sedimentary basins [4, 5] and the only method that reliably reaches several kilometres [9]. Near-surface bodies cause static shift, a frequency-independent offset of apparent resistivity that leaves phase unchanged [6]; TEM soundings are used to correct it [9]. Topography and lateral changes distort the fields [5], and MT sees large structures rather than single fractures [9].

On this map. Klang 1970-1979 (Afiq-Lot, Timna-Ramon, South Netanya, Tamar) and Rotstein 1979 (northern Israel); deepest models, to several km.

Controlled-source audio-frequency MT (CSAMT)

Principle. MT with an artificial source, a horizontal electric dipole on the surface, which avoids the weak natural signal [7]. Far from the source (the far field) Cagniard resistivity is computed from orthogonal E and H as in MT; in the near field and transition zone it is distorted and does not show true resistivity [7].

Measurement. At Afek a 1500 m grounded transmitter sat several km from 800 m receiver lines with 200 m dipoles, recording 8192-0.0625 Hz; scalar mode measures along-line Ex and across-line Hy [R3]. The in-line (end-on) layout was chosen as more sensitive to a deep resistive layer, seen as a "notch" in the transition zone [R3]. Results are 1-D smooth models of resistivity versus depth [R3].

Uses and limits. Used in mineral, engineering and hydrology surveys; a recent field example reached about 50 m to 2 km [7]. Source-receiver distance controls signal strength and which zone a reading falls in [7].

On this map. Afek 2013, southern Golan (247 sites).

Time-domain electromagnetics (TDEM / TEM)

Principle. Current in a transmitter loop is switched on and off. Each fast switch-off induces eddy currents in the ground (Faraday's law) whose strength depends on resistivity; their decaying secondary magnetic field is measured at the surface [1]. No electrodes are needed, and depth is set by the time after switch-off, not by the transmitter-receiver distance [1, 8].

Measurement. In the common central-loop array a receiver coil sits inside a square loop and targets two to three times the loop size can be detected [1]. Late-time apparent resistivities are inverted to 1-D layered models [1]. Long-offset TEM (LOTEM) uses a grounded line source far from the receivers for deep work [1].

Uses and limits. Best for conductive targets, with very good vertical resolution [8]; widely used to map groundwater salinity and saline bodies from tens of metres to kilometres [1]. Best lateral resolution of the 1-D sounding methods, works on almost any terrain and is little affected by neighbouring blocks; the drawbacks are a fast-decaying, noisy signal and costly equipment [1]. Unaffected by near-surface static shift [9].

On this map. Goldman 2000, coastal aquifer (seawater interface, about 100-200 m).
